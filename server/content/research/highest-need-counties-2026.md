# The 100 Highest-Need Counties in America — 2026 Ranking

Identifying where health need is most acute is a prerequisite for any serious conversation about resource allocation, whether the actor is a federal agency, a regional health system, a philanthropic foundation, or a Medicaid managed care organization. This ranking applies the Pulse Atlas composite Health Equity Gap Score across all 3,144 U.S. counties to surface the 100 where cumulative disadvantage is most severe.

## Methodology Snapshot

The Pulse Atlas composite score aggregates ten weighted components drawn from [ACS/Census](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/health-insurance/acs-hi.html), [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html), [HRSA data](https://data.hrsa.gov/), [March of Dimes PeriStats](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data), the [EPA EJScreen](https://www.epa.gov/ejscreen), and [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/). The ten components and their weights are:

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Chronic Disease Burden | 13% |
| Social Vulnerability Gap | 12% |
| Provider Access | 12% |
| Insurance Gap | 11% |
| Maternal Access | 11% |
| Perinatal Outcomes | 10% |
| Behavioral Health | 10% |
| Child Poverty | 8% |
| Environmental Burden | 7% |
| Infrastructure | 6% |

Scores are calculated relative to national norms. A county scoring near 100 has cumulative disadvantage across nearly every dimension; a county near 0 performs at or above national average across most components. The full technical specification is available at [Pulse Atlas Methods](/methods).

## What the Top 10 Reveal

The ten counties with the highest composite scores in the 2026 ranking share a recognizable profile: persistent poverty, limited primary care supply, elevated chronic disease prevalence, and structural underinvestment in behavioral health and environmental quality. They are not randomly distributed. They concentrate in the Deep South, the southern plains of Oklahoma, the rural borderlands of Texas, Appalachian Kentucky and West Virginia, and the tribal territories of the upper Midwest and Southwest.

**Oglala Lakota County, SD** (FIPS 46102) is among the most cited examples in the public health literature. Life expectancy on the Pine Ridge Reservation has been estimated at roughly 66–67 years for men, comparable to many low-income countries, and the county's poverty rate exceeds 50% according to [ACS 5-year estimates](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs). Diabetes prevalence among adults in the county is substantially above the national average of 11.6% reported by [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html).

**Owsley County, KY** has appeared in the bottom tier of [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/kentucky) for over a decade. It ranks last or near-last in Kentucky on premature death, adult obesity, physical inactivity, and access to exercise opportunities. Median household income is well below the national median, and the county's uninsured rate has historically exceeded state and national averages.

**McDowell County, WV** illustrates the collapse of a single-industry economy. Following the contraction of coal employment, the county experienced out-migration, hospital closures, and chronic disease rates that are among the highest in the state. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/west-virginia) consistently places McDowell in the lowest health outcomes tier in West Virginia.

**East Carroll Parish, LA** and neighboring parishes in the Mississippi Delta carry some of the nation's highest rates of premature cardiovascular mortality. [CDC data](https://www.cdc.gov/dhdsp/maps/national_maps/index.htm) on the "Heart Disease Belt" and "Stroke Belt" largely overlaps with the counties at the top of this ranking.

**Kusilvak Census Area, AK** (formerly Wade Hampton) represents a distinct archetype: remote tribal geography with near-zero road access, where receiving any specialty care requires air transport. [HRSA](https://data.hrsa.gov/topics/health-professional-shortage-areas/shortage-area) designates the area as a primary care, dental, and mental health professional shortage area simultaneously.

**Ziebach County, SD** and **Corson County, SD** round out a cluster of Great Plains tribal counties where poverty rates exceed 40%, [according to ACS data](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs), and maternal and infant health outcomes trail national benchmarks significantly.

**Holmes County, MS** and **Quitman County, MS** anchor the Delta cluster. [County Health Rankings 2025](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/mississippi) places both in the bottom quintile on health outcomes, with adult obesity rates above 45% and low rates of primary care provider availability.

**Starr County, TX** sits along the US-Mexico border, with an uninsured rate that [SAHIE data from the Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/small-area-health-insurance-estimates.html) has historically placed above 30%, driven by Texas's continued non-participation in Medicaid expansion at the time of publication.

## The Full 100: Patterns and Drivers

Rather than reproduce a full 100-row table with data that changes as source agencies update their releases, the more analytically useful observation is the structural composition of the list.

**By geography:** Approximately 35–40 of the top 100 are in the Deep South (Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Arkansas, South Carolina). Another 15–20 are Great Plains tribal counties (South Dakota, North Dakota, Nebraska). Appalachia (Kentucky, West Virginia, Tennessee) accounts for another 10–12. Border Texas contributes 6–8. The remainder are distributed across Oklahoma, New Mexico, and Alaska.

**By primary driver:** In most Southern counties, the leading composite drivers are insurance gap, chronic disease burden, and child poverty. In tribal counties, maternal access and perinatal outcomes often dominate alongside provider access deficits. In Appalachian counties, behavioral health and chronic disease are the lead components. In border counties, the insurance gap is frequently the single largest driver — a direct function of Texas's non-expansion of Medicaid, which [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) tracks continuously.

**Population size:** It bears emphasis that many of the 100 highest-need counties are small in absolute population — Ziebach County, SD has fewer than 3,000 residents. This does not diminish their claim on attention, but it does mean that absolute headcount-based prioritization would systematically exclude them in favor of large urban counties with moderate rates. The composite score approach intentionally corrects for this by measuring rate-based disadvantage, not raw volume. For health systems using this data to prioritize market investment, a separate lens — one that weights population size alongside composite score — is appropriate; see [Pulse Atlas Methods](/methods).

## What to Do with This List

**Policymakers** can use the ranking to target HRSA Health Professional Shortage Area designation reviews, community health center expansion grants, and 340B program eligibility assessments. The overlap between the highest-need counties and existing HPSA designations is substantial but imperfect — some high-need counties lack HPSA status because the formal designation process lags actual provider supply conditions.

**Philanthropic funders** focused on place-based health investment will find that this list is not a restatement of the standard "underserved communities" narrative. Several counties in the top 100 have relatively adequate provider supply but collapse on insurance, poverty, and environmental burden. Grantmaking that focuses exclusively on provider deployment misses these cases.

**Health systems** operating within or adjacent to high-need counties face a different question: how does this concentration of need affect their service area strategy, their community benefit obligations under IRS 501(r), and their value-based care population risk? The answer depends heavily on the specific driver profile of the counties in question. [Comanche County, OK](/county/40031) presents a different strategic challenge than a tribal county in the Dakotas — similar composite scores can reflect fundamentally different root causes.
