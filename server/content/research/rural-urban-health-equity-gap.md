# The Rural-Urban Health Equity Gap Isn't What You Think

The conventional framing of rural health disparities is a story about remoteness — thin provider networks, long travel times, and communities too small to support a hospital. That framing is not wrong, but it is incomplete in ways that consistently mislead resource allocation decisions. A county-by-county analysis of health equity gap scores reveals a more disaggregated and analytically useful picture: health disadvantage clusters into at least four distinct archetypes, and two of them have little to do with geographic isolation.

## The Misread: Rural Versus Urban Is the Wrong Dichotomy

The [CDC's rural-urban classification scheme](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data_access/urban_rural.htm) runs from large central metropolitan (code 1) to noncore rural (code 6). A reasonable expectation — one embedded in most policy discussions — is that health outcomes worsen monotonically as you move from code 1 to code 6. The data does not support a clean version of that story.

[CDC analyses of chronic disease burden](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/51967/cdc_51967_DS1.pdf) confirm that rural residents carry higher rates of COPD, diabetes, hypertension, and obesity than their urban counterparts. [County Health Rankings 2025](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) similarly documents that premature death rates are higher in nonmetropolitan areas. But when you move from health outcomes to health equity gap scores — which capture the structural conditions that produce those outcomes — the geographic distribution becomes far more complex.

Several mid-size metropolitan counties (codes 3 and 4 in the CDC classification, populations between 50,000 and 250,000) carry composite scores comparable to the most disadvantaged noncore rural counties. Conversely, many genuinely remote rural counties perform relatively well on composite measures because their populations, though sparse, have decent insurance coverage and minimal environmental burden.

The binary rural-urban frame also obscures substantial heterogeneity within each category. A ranching county in Wyoming faces fundamentally different structural conditions than a farming county in the Mississippi Delta, even if both are classified as "rural" by standard definitions.

## Four Archetypes That Actually Explain the Data

### 1. Deep Rural South

These counties — concentrated in Mississippi, Alabama, Louisiana, Georgia, and South Carolina — combine high rates of chronic disease, persistent multi-generational poverty, and inadequate insurance coverage. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) consistently places the bottom-ranked counties in Mississippi (Holmes, Quitman, Humphreys, Sharkey) and Alabama (Lowndes, Wilcox, Perry) in the bottom decile nationally on health outcomes.

What distinguishes this archetype is not remoteness per se — many of these counties are within 60–90 minutes of a metropolitan area — but rather the layering of disadvantages: non-expansion of Medicaid in states like Mississippi and Alabama (the [KFF Medicaid expansion tracker](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) documents which states have and have not expanded as of the publication date), child poverty rates above 35%, and chronic disease prevalence far exceeding national norms. [CDC PLACES data](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) indicates adult obesity rates above 45% and diabetes prevalence above 18% in some Delta counties, roughly double the national averages.

The insurance gap component is especially pronounced here. Adults in non-expansion states are twice as likely to be uninsured as those in expansion states, according to [CDC National Health Statistics data](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr194.pdf). This structural difference means that otherwise identical health conditions translate into worse outcomes in non-expansion Deep South counties, because the population delays or forgoes care.

### 2. Tribal Territories

The counties with the most extreme composite scores in the nation are disproportionately tribal. Oglala Lakota County (SD), Ziebach County (SD), Corson County (SD), Menominee County (WI), Big Horn County (MT), and McKinley County (NM) share a profile that is distinct from either the Deep South or Appalachian archetypes.

The distinguishing features are the coexistence of extreme poverty — [ACS 5-year estimates](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs) show poverty rates above 40–50% in some Pine Ridge area counties — with the specific maternal and infant health crisis documented by [March of Dimes](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data). Approximately 1 in 5 births among American Indian/Alaska Native women occur in counties without full access to maternity care, according to [March of Dimes 2024 data](https://www.marchofdimes.org/maternity-care-deserts-report). The perinatal outcomes and maternal access components of the Pulse Atlas composite score are the lead drivers in most tribal counties.

Geographic isolation is a genuine factor here — unlike the Deep South archetype, many of these counties are genuinely remote — but the legal and jurisdictional complexity of Indian Health Service funding, tribal sovereignty, and federal trust responsibility creates an additional layer of structural disadvantage that pure distance measures do not capture.

### 3. Post-Industrial Mid-Metro

This archetype surprises many analysts: several medium-sized metropolitan counties — former manufacturing centers in the industrial Midwest and mid-Atlantic — score in the bottom quintile on health equity despite having populations of 100,000 to 400,000 and nominally adequate provider supply.

The mechanism is not provider scarcity. It is the confluence of post-industrial poverty, high rates of substance use disorder, behavioral health system undercapacity, and elevated exposure to environmental hazards from legacy industrial sites. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) data shows that counties like Vermilion County (IL), Fayette County (IN), and Baltimore City (MD) — all medium or large urban — rank at or near the bottom of their states on health outcomes, driven primarily by premature death, poor mental health days, and drug overdose mortality.

[CDC drug overdose data](https://www.cdc.gov/drugoverdose/data/index.html) confirms that overdose death rates are not exclusively a rural phenomenon; several mid-size Ohio, Indiana, and West Virginia metropolitan counties post rates above 50 per 100,000, exceeding many rural counties. The behavioral health component of the composite score, which carries a 10% weight, reflects this reality.

This archetype is strategically important for regional health systems because it means that institutions operating in mid-sized industrial markets often face equity gaps comparable to those in remote rural areas — without the policy sympathy that rural framing tends to generate.

### 4. Border Colonias

The colonias along the Texas-Mexico border represent a fourth distinct archetype. Counties like Starr, Zavala, Dimmit, and Jim Hogg combine extremely high uninsured rates — driven by Texas's non-expansion of Medicaid, large undocumented immigrant populations, and low employer-sponsored coverage rates — with limited provider infrastructure and high rates of diabetes and hypertension rooted in dietary patterns and limited preventive care access.

[SAHIE data from the Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/small-area-health-insurance-estimates.html) has historically shown uninsured rates in the 25–40% range in some border Texas counties, among the highest in the nation. [KFF](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/key-facts-about-the-uninsured-population/) notes that Texas's overall uninsured rate of 19.2% is nearly double the national rate of 9.8% as of 2024. Within Texas, the border counties are significantly worse than that statewide figure.

Unlike the Deep Rural South, where poverty and uninsurance are the dominant drivers but provider supply has modestly improved over the last decade, border colonias often face simultaneous deficits across insurance, provider access, infrastructure, and environmental burden. The composite score reflects this stacking.

## Why the Archetype Framework Matters for Strategy

The rural-urban binary is not merely analytically imprecise; it has practical consequences.

Policy instruments designed around geographic remoteness — rural health clinic designations, HRSA rural health grants, critical access hospital payment status — do not reach the post-industrial mid-metro or Deep South archetypes well. A county's NCHS urban-rural classification code does not determine its composite equity gap, and resource allocation systems that treat that code as a proxy for need will systematically misplace resources.

For health systems engaged in community health needs assessment under IRS 501(r) requirements, the archetype framework provides a more defensible analytical foundation than the standard rural-versus-urban comparison. The county pages at [Franklin County, WA](/county/53021) and [Natrona County, WY](/county/56025) illustrate how similar-appearing geographies can carry very different driver profiles when the full 10-component composite is examined. [Idaho County, ID](/county/16049) and [Ada County, ID](/county/16001) show how even within a single state, the urban-rural distinction can obscure more than it reveals.

The policy implication is not that rural health programs are misguided — rural areas carry real, documented disadvantages across multiple domains. It is that the rural frame is insufficient as the organizing principle for health equity investment, and that archetype-aware analysis produces better targeting decisions.
