# Idaho's Health Equity Story: Why Rural Idaho Ranks Where It Does

Idaho occupies a paradoxical position in American health equity analysis. It is a state that expanded Medicaid via ballot initiative — voters approved Proposition 2 in 2018, with enrollment beginning in January 2020 — yet it maintains a health system that is heavily rural, regionally isolated, and structurally under-resourced outside of its two main urban centers. That combination produces a distinctive county-level pattern: a handful of well-performing urban and amenity-economy counties, surrounded by a broad range of rural counties with significant but varied health equity gaps.

## Statewide Overview

Idaho's 44 counties range from Ada County (Boise) — the most populous and economically dynamic — to counties with populations under 1,000, no hospital, and no full-time physician. The state's overall health performance is mixed: [America's Health Rankings](https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/HealthInsurance/ID) places Idaho 38th among states on health insurance coverage as of 2024, reflecting the substantial uninsured rates in several rural counties that predate and postdate the Medicaid expansion.

[Idaho Rural Health](https://id.rhtcompass.com/) data shows that 11 of Idaho's 44 counties (25%) have no hospital, and the state averages 5.3 primary care physicians per 10,000 population — below national norms. [HRSA](https://data.hrsa.gov/) has designated 90 primary care Health Professional Shortage Areas, 85 dental HPSAs, and 102 mental health HPSAs within the state, representing an estimated 4.8 million underserved population-equivalents.

On the insurance front, Medicaid expansion has substantially reduced uninsured rates in many Idaho counties. [ACS 5-year estimates](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho) show wide variation: Ada County (3.3%), Latah County (4.9%), and Blaine County (10.3%) span the insurance spectrum within the state. However, Clearwater County (26.4%), Owyhee County (21.1%), and Gooding County (19.6%) remain among the highest-uninsured counties in the Mountain West despite expansion — indicating that coverage eligibility alone does not translate to enrollment in the most isolated and economically disadvantaged communities.

Idaho's chronic disease profile reflects its mix of relatively active outdoor recreation culture in some communities and sedentary agricultural or resource-extraction economies in others. [Idaho County health data](https://idahostateauthority.com/counties/idaho-county/health/) shows adult obesity rates around 32.6% and adult smoking at 17.7% in Idaho County — consistent with national rural averages but substantially above the rates in Blaine County (Sun Valley area) and Ada County.

## The Five Highest-Need Counties

### Clearwater County

Clearwater County posts a composite gap score of **42.2** with a 10.1% uninsured rate, 31.8% adult obesity, and a life expectancy of 75.9 years — a middle-of-the-pack profile whose real story is provider thinness (0 OB providers per 10K, 135 PCPs per 100K) rather than uninsurance ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/16035))

Clearwater County carries the highest uninsured rate in Idaho at 26.4% according to [ACS 5-year estimates](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho). It is a noncore rural county in north-central Idaho with a timber-dependent economy, limited provider availability, and a geographic profile that makes accessing regional medical centers in Lewiston or Spokane feasible but burdensome. Despite Medicaid expansion, the persistently high uninsured rate suggests significant enrollment barriers — likely a combination of administrative complexity, lack of trusted navigators, and a population skeptical of public programs.

### Owyhee County

Owyhee County scores **63.0** on the composite — one of the highest in Idaho — driven by a 19.4% uninsured rate, a maternity care desert designation, a 60.8-mile average distance to a hospital, and an SVI in the 74th percentile ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/16073))

Owyhee County in southwestern Idaho borders Nevada and Oregon at the edge of the high desert. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/idaho) has historically placed Owyhee County near the bottom of Idaho counties on health outcomes. Its uninsured rate of 21.1% is the second highest in the state. The county has a substantial Hispanic agricultural population in communities like Homedale and Marsing, where language access barriers complicate insurance enrollment and care-seeking. [Becker's Hospital Review](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/rankings-and-ratings/the-unhealthiest-county-in-each-us-state/) identified Owyhee County as the unhealthiest county in Idaho in a prior ranking cycle based on County Health Rankings data.

### Power County

Power County posts a composite of **54.1**, with 14.4% uninsured, 38.0% adult obesity, teen birth rate of 24.9 per 1,000, and a socioeconomic SVI in the 77th percentile ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/16077))

Power County has an uninsured rate of 16.9% and sits in eastern Idaho's agricultural belt. The county is home to significant potato and grain farming operations that employ seasonal workers, a population segment with historically low rates of employer-sponsored coverage and inconsistent Medicaid enrollment. Its poverty rate and child poverty rate are elevated relative to the state median.

### Gooding County

Gooding County scores **58.8**, with a 17.9% uninsured rate, a maternity care desert designation, an OB unit closure, and a teen birth rate of 30.2 per 1,000 — the highest in this cluster ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/16047))

Gooding County's 19.6% uninsured rate reflects the central Idaho agricultural economy and its associated workforce dynamics. The county has limited local provider supply and is dependent on Twin Falls — approximately 30 miles east — for most specialized care. [ACS data](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho) and [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/idaho) both flag insurance gap and provider access as co-primary drivers.

### Idaho County

[Pulse Atlas profile: /county/16049]

Idaho County represents the large-geography challenge: it is among the largest counties by area in the contiguous United States, with vast roadless wilderness areas that make its geographic extent misleading as a measure of access. [Health By County](https://healthbycounty.com/idaho/idaho-county) gives Idaho County a health score of 55/100 and ranks it 24th of 44 Idaho counties — squarely average for the state but below national benchmarks on key metrics including provider access. Overdose death rates are rising, [County Health Rankings data](https://idahostateauthority.com/counties/idaho-county/health/) showing a trend that tracks the national pattern in rural, post-industrial communities. Mental health provider ratios are well below national norms.

## The Three Best-Performing Counties

### Ada County

[Pulse Atlas profile: /county/16001]

Ada County anchors Idaho's Treasure Valley metropolitan area and houses Boise, the state's economic and medical center. It is home to [St. Luke's Health System](https://www.stlukesonline.org/) and [Saint Alphonsus Regional Medical Center](https://www.saintalphonsus.org/), the two dominant health systems in the state. The uninsured rate is 3.3% — among the lowest in the state and approaching New England levels of coverage. [ACS data](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho) and [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/idaho) consistently place Ada County in the top three or four in Idaho on health outcomes. Provider supply is the best in the state, the labor market supports employer-sponsored insurance, and the population is younger and more educated than the state median.

Ada County's performance illustrates a point about composite health equity scoring: even the best-performing Idaho county carries moderate gap scores on some components because the state baseline is itself challenged. The county's relative position within Idaho is strong, but its national ranking is more modest.

### Blaine County

[Pulse Atlas profile: /county/16013]

Blaine County (Sun Valley, Hailey) represents the amenity-economy archetype: a high-income, high-education mountain county that attracts medical professionals and well-insured residents while also harboring a service-sector workforce with elevated uninsured rates. The county's health outcomes ranking is strong — [Indicators Idaho](http://indicatorsidaho.org/DrawRegion.aspx?PageID=10&RegionID=16013&IndicatorID=100041) places Blaine County 3rd in health outcomes and 7th in health factors among Idaho's 44 counties.

The 10.3% uninsured rate, while moderate by national standards, is elevated for such a high-income county and reflects the workforce dynamics of resort economies: seasonal workers, employees in food service and hospitality, and small business operators often lack employer-sponsored coverage.

### Latah County

[Pulse Atlas profile: /county/16057]

Latah County houses Moscow and the University of Idaho, making it Idaho's primary college-town county. The university presence produces a younger, better-educated population with relatively higher rates of employer-sponsored and student coverage. The uninsured rate of 4.9% reflects this structural advantage. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/idaho) shows Latah in the upper half of Idaho counties on most health factors, anchored by lower smoking rates, better access to exercise opportunities, and a more educated population base.

## What's Driving the Gap

Three structural factors explain the majority of Idaho's within-state health equity variation:

**Insurance enrollment barriers post-expansion.** Medicaid expansion reduced the number of Idahoans eligible but uninsured, but it did not eliminate uninsurance in rural counties. The counties with the highest persistent uninsured rates — Clearwater, Owyhee, Gooding, Power — share profiles of agricultural or timber economies with mobile workforces, limited outreach infrastructure, and populations with historically low rates of government program participation. Closing the enrollment gap requires on-the-ground navigator infrastructure, not just eligibility expansion.

**Provider supply concentration.** The [Idaho rural hospital data](https://id.rhtcompass.com/) showing 11 counties without any hospital is a structural fact that Medicaid expansion and private insurance do not address. Provider supply requires sustained investment in workforce pipeline, loan repayment programs, and critical access hospital financial support — instruments that operate through HRSA and state policy, not market forces alone.

**Behavioral health system capacity.** [HRSA's 102 mental health HPSAs](https://id.rhtcompass.com/) in Idaho represent the most severe relative shortage — more than the primary care or dental HPSAs. Rural Idaho counties show rising overdose mortality, and the [Idaho County data](https://idahostateauthority.com/counties/idaho-county/health/) confirms that this trend is not confined to the most rural jurisdictions. Building behavioral health capacity in a state where provider supply is already constrained requires creative solutions — telehealth, community health workers, school-based programs — that go beyond traditional clinic deployment.
