# Where the Uninsured Actually Live: 2026 County Rankings

The U.S. uninsured rate is not a single number. It is a weighted average of deeply heterogeneous county-level conditions that range from under 3% in well-insured suburban counties to above 30% in the poorest corners of non-expansion states. Understanding where uninsurance actually concentrates — and why — is prerequisite analysis for any strategy that depends on financial access to care, whether the user is a health system, a Medicaid managed care organization, a community health center network, or a state policy office.

## The National Baseline

[KFF's June 2026 analysis of uninsured rates](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/key-facts-about-the-uninsured-population/) documents that for the first time since 2019, the number of people without health coverage increased in 2024: 26.7 million people ages 0–64 were uninsured, an increase of more than 1.3 million from 2023. The national uninsured rate for the under-65 population rose from 9.5% to 9.8%.

This reversal followed the end of continuous Medicaid enrollment protections that had held the uninsured rate to historic lows during and immediately after the COVID-19 public health emergency. The unwinding of those protections beginning in 2023 resulted in Medicaid disenrollment for millions, not all of whom secured alternative coverage.

[Census Bureau SAHIE data](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/small-area-health-insurance-estimates.html) shows that between 2022 and 2023, uninsured rates decreased in 194 counties and increased in 85 counties. As of 2023, 1,455 counties (46.3%) had estimated uninsured rates below 10%.

## The Medicaid Expansion Overlay

No variable explains county-level uninsured rate variation more powerfully than state Medicaid expansion status. [KFF's Medicaid expansion tracker](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) identifies the states that have and have not expanded Medicaid under the ACA as of the publication date.

[CDC National Health Statistics data](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr194.pdf) shows the gap clearly: adults ages 18–64 in non-expansion states were twice as likely to be uninsured (19.6%) as those in Medicaid expansion states (9.1%). The expansion decision made at the state level translates into dramatic variation in county-level uninsured rates, because Medicaid eligibility thresholds determine whether low-income workers, parents, and adults without children can access public coverage.

Within the non-expansion holdouts — most prominently Texas and, until its 2023 expansion, South Dakota — the county-level uninsured rates reflect this policy choice most acutely. [KFF](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/key-facts-about-the-uninsured-population/) reports that Texas had the highest state uninsured rate at 19.2%, nearly double the national rate.

Twenty-four of the top 25 are in Texas — the only large state that has not expanded Medicaid, and one that also concentrates border colonias and agricultural-worker communities with employer-sponsored coverage gaps. The single non-Texas county in the top five, Holmes County, Ohio, reflects a different mechanism: a large Amish/Mennonite population that opts out of conventional insurance ([HHS Office of Minority Health](https://minorityhealth.hhs.gov/)).

The geographic concentration in border Texas is striking but explicable. These counties combine three reinforcing factors: Texas's non-expansion of Medicaid, a large agricultural workforce with limited employer-sponsored coverage, and substantial undocumented immigrant populations who are categorically ineligible for most public coverage regardless of state expansion status.

Holmes County, Ohio is a distinct case. Its high uninsured rate reflects the Amish community's traditional practice of self-insuring, which is treated statistically as uninsured in coverage surveys even though the community maintains informal risk-pooling mechanisms. This illustrates the hazard of applying universal policy solutions to uniform-appearing data.

## What Changed Since the ACA

The Affordable Care Act's insurance expansion mechanisms — Medicaid expansion, the health insurance marketplace, and the individual mandate — produced the largest reduction in the uninsured rate in U.S. history when they took full effect in 2014–2015. [Census Bureau data](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2022/09/uninsured-rate-declined-in-28-states.html) documents that the uninsured rate declined in 28 states between 2019 and 2021 alone.

The pre-ACA baseline (circa 2012–2013) showed a national uninsured rate of approximately 15–16%, compared to the post-ACA low of approximately 7.7% in early 2023 as measured by [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr194.pdf). For county-level analysis, the most dramatic changes occurred in Medicaid expansion states where low-income adults gained eligibility. [Research in Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/43/7/1449/35537/) found that in expansion states, uninsured rates declined by 20 percentage points among adults at or below 138% of the federal poverty level between 2012 and 2016, compared to 13 percentage points in non-expansion states.

The ACA's impact on county-level uninsured rates was thus heavily mediated by state-level policy choices. Counties in expansion states — including Idaho, which [expanded Medicaid in 2020 via ballot initiative](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) — saw substantially larger uninsured rate reductions than comparable counties in non-expansion states.

The 2023–2024 Medicaid unwinding represents the first significant reversal of that trend. States that conducted aggressive redeterminations — checking eligibility for all enrollees who had received continuous coverage protections — saw larger increases in their uninsured rates. The [Census Bureau's September 2025 data release](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/09/uninsured-rates.html) showed uninsured rates increasing in 18 states and the District of Columbia between 2022 and 2024.

## Idaho, Wyoming, and the Mountain West

Idaho presents an instructive case for the intersection of Medicaid expansion, rural geography, and county-level uninsured variation. Prior to its 2020 Medicaid expansion, Idaho had among the higher uninsured rates in the Mountain West. Post-expansion, [ACS 5-year data](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho) shows substantial variation across the state's 44 counties, from Gem County (0.1%) to Clearwater County (26.4%).

Several Idaho counties — including [Blaine County](/county/16013), [Valley County](/county/16085), and [Latah County](/county/16057) — occupy intermediate uninsured rates that reflect the mix of seasonal workers, retirees, self-employed individuals, and university populations that characterize mountain and college-town economies. The full county-level profiles at the individual Pulse Atlas pages document these differences in detail.

Wyoming has not expanded Medicaid as of the publication date. [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) tracks Wyoming's ongoing status. The practical consequence is visible in [Natrona County's](/county/56025) insurance gap component score — which captures the gap between the observed uninsured rate and the rate that would likely prevail under expansion. For health systems operating in Wyoming, the insurance gap is a structural condition that affects revenue cycle, charity care volumes, and community benefit calculation.

## Why County-Level Granularity Matters

State-level uninsured rates, while useful for policy comparison, mask the within-state variation that drives practical strategy. Within Texas, the difference between a suburban Houston county and a border colonia county may be 25 or more percentage points in uninsured rate. Within Idaho, the difference between Ada County and Clearwater County is comparable.

For health systems, this granularity matters in several ways. Community benefit planning under IRS 501(r) requires identification of community need, and the uninsured rate is typically the starting point. Service line expansion decisions depend partly on the payer mix in a target market. Population health programs under value-based contracts need to account for the proportion of the attributed population that lacks insurance continuity.

The [Pulse Atlas Insurance Gap component](/methods), weighted at 11% of the composite score, translates county-level uninsured rates into a standardized gap measure relative to national benchmarks. For [Ada County, ID](/county/16001) — a relatively well-insured urban county — the insurance gap component contributes minimally to the composite. For [Comanche County, OK](/county/40031), the insurance gap is a more significant driver of the composite score.
