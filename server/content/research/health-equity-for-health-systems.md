# A CFO's Guide to Health Equity Data: Using County-Level Metrics for Strategic Planning

Health equity has moved from the periphery of health system strategy to its center — not because of a values shift alone, but because the data infrastructure now exists to make it analytically tractable. County-level composite measures that were not available five years ago can now be integrated into market analysis, community benefit planning, value-based care population management, and capital allocation decisions.

This article is written for health system CFOs, Chief Strategy Officers, and population health leaders who need to operationalize health equity data within existing planning frameworks. It draws on the Pulse Atlas county-level Health Equity Gap Score and its ten component measures to illustrate four specific use cases where this data type generates decision-relevant insight that other standard market data does not.

## Why Standard Market Data Is Insufficient

Traditional health system market analysis relies on service area definitions, patient origin data, discharge volumes, and payer mix analysis. These tools are excellent for understanding current utilization patterns. They are poor predictors of future need in markets undergoing structural change, and they systematically underweight populations that are currently out of the health system — the uninsured, the underinsured, and those with undiagnosed or unmanaged chronic conditions.

A hospital CFO looking at a county with 15% uninsured and high chronic disease prevalence sees it primarily as a payer mix challenge: elevated charity care, lower commercial revenue per case, and Medicaid reimbursement gaps. The health equity lens reframes the same county as a population with high unmet need, high preventable utilization, and substantial opportunity for revenue recovery if coverage is expanded or managed care enrollment is achieved.

The distinction matters strategically because the policy environment is actively changing the payer mix in high-need counties. Medicaid expansion — still pending in some states, recently completed in others — can shift 50,000 to 200,000 newly enrolled Medicaid members into a health system's service area within 12–24 months of expansion. [KFF's Medicaid expansion tracker](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) shows the current status of expansion decisions. Health systems that have used county-level uninsured rate data to anticipate those shifts have been better positioned to invest in primary care capacity and managed care contracting ahead of the enrollment wave.

## Use Case 1: Service Line Expansion and Market Prioritization

The Pulse Atlas composite score and its ten component measures can be used to construct a demand-need overlay on top of traditional service area utilization data. The overlay answers a question that utilization data alone cannot: where is unmet need highest, and what type of need is it?

For a health system evaluating a de novo primary care clinic, the relevant data inputs are:
- **Insurance gap component:** What percentage of the target population is uninsured? How much of that uninsurance would resolve with Medicaid expansion or marketplace enrollment?
- **Provider access component:** How does the target county's provider-to-population ratio compare to national benchmarks? Is the gap primarily in primary care, specialty care, or both?
- **Chronic disease component:** What specific chronic disease burden exists in the target population? This determines the clinical mix and staffing model required.

For a health system considering obstetric service line investment, the maternal access component and perinatal outcomes component provide direct inputs. As documented in the [Pulse Atlas maternity care desert analysis](/research/maternity-care-deserts-2026), over 35% of U.S. counties lack basic obstetric services. [March of Dimes PeriStats data](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?top=23) provides county-level classifications. A health system in a state with high desert concentration — Oklahoma, South Dakota, Nebraska — may find that adjacent counties represent significant service line expansion opportunities if the payer mix economics can be made to work.

The strategic decision framework looks like this: high composite score + specific component driver + favorable payer mix trajectory = investment-grade opportunity. High composite score + unfavorable payer mix with no clear coverage expansion pathway = community benefit deployment, not service line investment.

For markets like [Natrona County, WY](/county/56025) and [Franklin County, WA](/county/53021), this framework produces different conclusions depending on the state policy environment and the specific services under consideration.

## Use Case 2: Community Benefit Reporting and IRS 501(r) Compliance

The IRS requires nonprofit hospitals to conduct Community Health Needs Assessments every three years and to adopt implementation strategies that respond to identified needs. The specific requirement, under Section 501(r) of the Internal Revenue Code, is that the CHNA must take into account input from the community and information from persons with special knowledge of or expertise in public health, and must make the assessment widely available to the public.

County-level composite health equity data substantially elevates the analytical quality of the CHNA process. The traditional CHNA relies on community surveys, focus groups, and state-level secondary data. Layering in county-level composite scores and component-level breakdowns — with [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) chronic disease estimates, [HRSA provider shortage data](https://data.hrsa.gov/topics/health-professional-shortage-areas/shortage-area), [March of Dimes maternity access classifications](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?top=23), and [Census Bureau poverty and insurance estimates](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/sahie.html) — allows the hospital to meet the secondary data requirement with significantly more rigor than a state-level report allows.

The practical benefit extends beyond compliance. A CHNA that identifies specific county-level needs with supporting data provides the justification for community benefit expenditures that can be credited toward IRS requirements: charity care, Medicaid shortfall, community health improvement services, and subsidized health services. A health system that can document the magnitude and nature of the need in its service area counties can defend the scope and type of its community benefit program to board audit committees, bond rating agencies, and state health department overseers.

For health systems operating in high-need markets — including those with significant presence in counties like [Comanche County, OK](/county/40031) — the composite score and component data provide a defensible, data-driven foundation for CHNA conclusions that community survey data alone cannot deliver.

## Use Case 3: ACO Population Selection and Value-Based Care Risk Management

Accountable Care Organizations and other value-based care arrangements require managing the health of a defined attributed population. The financial performance of those programs depends substantially on the risk profile of the attributed population: higher-risk populations have higher expected costs, larger management investments, and — under two-sided risk arrangements — greater potential for both gain and loss.

County-level health equity data is predictive of population risk in several ways. The chronic disease component score, built from [CDC PLACES prevalence estimates](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html), is directly correlated with per-member medical cost for populations with those conditions. The social vulnerability component, drawing from [CDC SVI data](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/placeandhealth/svi/index.html), is predictive of care gap rates, preventable ED utilization, and medication adherence — all drivers of ACO performance.

The practical use case is risk stratification at the population planning level, not the individual patient level. Before a health system enters a new value-based arrangement — a CMMI model, a state Medicaid ACO, or a Medicare Shared Savings Program — it needs to characterize the expected risk of the attributed population. If the service area counties have high composite health equity gap scores, particularly on chronic disease and social vulnerability components, the risk adjustment methodology used in the contract needs to reflect that. Health systems that have underestimated population risk in ACO contracts — because they relied on historical utilization data rather than structural need data — have experienced significant financial losses in early performance periods.

Conversely, health systems that can invest in social needs navigation, chronic disease management, and behavioral health integration ahead of value-based contract launch — and can document the investment rationale using county-level need data — are in a stronger negotiating position with payers and with CMMI program officers.

## Use Case 4: Philanthropy Allocation and Health Equity Investment

The largest hospital systems in the United States control philanthropic endowments and make grants through affiliated foundations. Smaller systems participate in philanthropic decisions indirectly — through community benefit contributions, through relationships with community foundations, and through partnership with federally qualified health centers and other safety-net organizations.

County-level health equity data provides the targeting discipline for philanthropic investment that historically has relied on relationship networks and historical grant patterns. The question "where should we invest to achieve maximum health improvement per philanthropic dollar?" is answerable with data if the analytical framework is right.

The answer is not simply "invest in the highest-composite-score counties." Philanthropic dollars cannot substitute for structural policy change — Medicaid expansion, provider reimbursement reform, HPSA remediation — in the most severely disadvantaged counties. The more productive philanthropic targeting question is: which counties have moderate composite scores with specific, addressable component deficits where a targeted investment could move the needle within the grant period?

A county with a strong composite score except for a high behavioral health gap — and a clear community partner positioned to deliver integrated behavioral health services — may be a more productive philanthropic target than a county scoring poorly across all ten components where no single intervention can generate measurable change.

[ACS data on child poverty](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs) and the Pulse Atlas child poverty component provide one targeting layer for foundations focused on pediatric health. [HRSA HPSA data](https://data.hrsa.gov/topics/health-professional-shortage-areas/shortage-area) provides targeting for workforce investment philanthropy. The Pulse Atlas composite provides the integrating framework.

## Data Infrastructure Considerations

Health system planning teams implementing county-level health equity data should attend to several practical considerations.

**Data vintage:** The ten Pulse Atlas components draw from sources with different update frequencies. [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) updates annually; [ACS 5-year data](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs) carries a 2-year lag; [HRSA HPSA designations](https://data.hrsa.gov/topics/health-professional-shortage-areas/shortage-area) are updated on a rolling basis. Individual component scores may be 1–3 years old. Planning decisions for fast-moving markets — recent Medicaid expansion or hospital closure — should weight recency accordingly.

**Population denominators:** For small counties (under 10,000 population), sampling error in ACS estimates can be significant. The confidence intervals in SAHIE and ACS releases should inform analytic confidence placed in small-county data.

**Cross-county markets:** Most health systems serve multi-county markets. Aggregating county-level composite scores across a service area — weighted by population — produces a service area composite comparable to peer systems. The [Pulse Atlas Methods](/methods) page documents the aggregation methodology.

For county-level profiles on specific markets, including [Comanche County, OK](/county/40031), [Natrona County, WY](/county/56025), and [Franklin County, WA](/county/53021), the individual county profile pages provide component-level breakdowns and source data citations.
