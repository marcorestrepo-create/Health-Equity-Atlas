# Health Equity and Chronic Disease: The 13% Component in the Composite Score

Chronic disease is the largest single component of the Pulse Atlas Health Equity Gap Score, weighted at 13%. That weight reflects a consistent finding in county-level health research: the burden of preventable chronic conditions — type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, COPD, and cardiovascular disease — is both the most powerful predictor of premature mortality variation across counties and the dimension most directly responsive to the structural conditions that health equity interventions target.

This article examines the national geography of chronic disease burden at the county level, identifies the hotspot clusters where burden is most concentrated, and explains why the same chronic conditions manifest with such different intensity across similar-seeming geographies.

## The National Picture

[CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) provides small-area estimates for chronic disease prevalence at the county, city, census tract, and ZIP code levels. The national averages — against which county-level scores are benchmarked — are approximately:

- **Adult obesity:** ~32–33% of adults (CDC PLACES, most recent data cycle)
- **Type 2 diabetes:** ~11.6% of adults ([CDC National Diabetes Statistics Report](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/data/statistics-report/index.html))
- **Hypertension:** ~47% of adults ([CDC Vital Statistics](https://www.cdc.gov/bloodpressure/facts.htm))
- **COPD:** ~5.9–6.3% of adults ([CDC BRFSS](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/index.html))
- **Coronary heart disease:** ~6.2% of adults (CDC PLACES)

These averages mask county-level variation that spans roughly two- to threefold for most of these conditions. [CDC PLACES data](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) shows that adult obesity rates range from below 20% in some high-income suburban counties to above 50% in the highest-burden Mississippi Delta counties. Diabetes prevalence ranges from under 6% to above 20%.

The geographic distribution of high-burden counties is not random. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) and [CDC chronic disease mapping tools](https://www.cdc.gov/dhdsp/maps/national_maps/index.htm) document the "Diabetes Belt" (a crescent of high-prevalence counties through the Deep South and southern Appalachia) and the "Stroke Belt" (similar geographic footprint) as persistent geographic clusters that have not dissolved despite decades of public health intervention.

## Why Counties Vary: The Structural Explanation

The intuitive explanation for county-level chronic disease variation — that it reflects individual lifestyle choices — is both partially correct and fundamentally incomplete. A large body of research documents that chronic disease prevalence is strongly predicted by structural conditions that are not primarily under individual control:

**Food environment.** [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) includes the Food Environment Index, which measures access to healthy foods and rates of food insecurity. Counties with low food environment scores — characterized by food deserts (limited grocery access) and food swamps (high density of fast food relative to grocery stores) — show substantially higher rates of obesity and type 2 diabetes, independent of income. [CDC research](https://www.cdc.gov/nccdphp/dnpa/obesity/state_programs/index.htm) documents this relationship at the county level.

**Physical activity infrastructure.** Access to parks, trails, recreational facilities, and safe walking environments predicts physical activity rates. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) data on access to exercise opportunities shows strong correlation with obesity and cardiovascular disease rates at the county level. A county like [Blaine County, ID](/county/16013) — with exceptional outdoor recreation access — shows lower obesity rates than nearby counties with similar income levels but fewer activity opportunities.

**Insurance coverage and preventive care access.** Chronic disease detected early, managed consistently, and treated with evidence-based protocols produces fewer complications and less premature mortality. Counties with high uninsured rates — like those in non-expansion states documented in the [Pulse Atlas uninsured analysis](/research/uninsured-by-county-2026) — show worse chronic disease management outcomes even when raw prevalence is comparable to better-insured counties. [Research in Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/43/7/1449/35537/) documents that Medicaid expansion was associated with significantly reduced uninsured rates in the "Diabetes Belt" and improved disease management outcomes.

**Historical and structural racism.** The geographic overlap between the most chronic-disease-burdened counties and the historical geography of slavery, sharecropping, and racial economic exclusion in the Deep South is not coincidental. [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/) has expanded its equity-focused measures to capture the role of structural racism in health outcomes, documenting that Black Americans in particular face chronic disease burdens exceeding what income alone predicts.

## Hotspot Counties and Clusters

### The Mississippi Delta

The contiguous cluster of counties in the Mississippi Delta — including Holmes, Quitman, Humphreys, Sharkey, and Issaquena counties in Mississippi — consistently posts the highest rates of adult obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease mortality in the nation. [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) county-level estimates show adult obesity above 45% and diabetes above 18% in multiple Delta counties. These counties have limited grocery infrastructure, high poverty rates, historically inadequate provider supply, and some of the highest rates of food insecurity in the country.

In Holmes County, Mississippi, the diabetes rate reaches **21.0%**, hypertension **53.1%**, and adult obesity **51.5%** — roughly double national averages, with a life expectancy of 65.7 years and food insecurity at 24.6% ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/28051))

### Appalachian COPD Belt

COPD prevalence is geographically concentrated in a band running through eastern Kentucky, West Virginia, southern Ohio, and Tennessee — the historical coalfields. [CDC data](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/51967/cdc_51967_DS1.pdf) documents COPD prevalence ranging from 3.2% to 15.6% across U.S. counties, with the highest quartile (8.5–15.6%) concentrated in nonmetropolitan areas of Kentucky, West Virginia, Ohio, Tennessee, Alabama, and Arkansas. The combination of occupational exposure (mining, logging), high smoking rates, and limited pulmonology access drives this concentration.

Owsley County, KY — consistently among the worst-ranked counties nationally on health outcomes — has a COPD burden substantially above the national average, according to [County Health Rankings](https://www.countyhealthrankings.org/health-data/kentucky).

### Southern Plains and Oklahoma

Oklahoma has ranked among the bottom ten states on multiple health measures for years. [America's Health Rankings](https://www.americashealthrankings.org) placed Oklahoma among the lowest-performing states on overall health, with the state's high prevalence of smoking, uninsured population, and premature death rate as leading contributors. [Comanche County, OK](/county/40031) — one of the top-impression counties in the Pulse Atlas user base — shows chronic disease burden consistent with this statewide profile, with adult obesity and diabetes rates above national averages.

In Comanche County, Oklahoma, adult obesity sits at **43.7%**, hypertension at **37.7%**, and diabetes at **12.7%** — with heart disease prevalence at 6.4% and life expectancy of 73.2 years ([Pulse Atlas](https://www.thepulseatlas.com/county/40031))

### Great Plains Tribal Counties

The tribal counties of the Dakotas, Montana, and the Southwest combine chronic disease burdens that are among the most severe in the country with provider access deficits that limit management of those conditions. [Oglala Lakota County, SD](https://www.healthdata.org/research-analysis/gbd) has diabetes prevalence estimates well above the national average. American Indian/Alaska Native adults have consistently higher rates of type 2 diabetes than any other racial group in the United States, according to [CDC National Diabetes Statistics data](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/data/statistics-report/index.html), reflecting both genetic predisposition and the structural determinants — food environment, poverty, historical trauma — that drive behavioral risk factors.

## The Interaction Between Chronic Disease and Other Components

Chronic disease does not operate in isolation within the Pulse Atlas composite score. It interacts with and reinforces other components:

**Chronic disease + insurance gap.** The combination of high chronic disease prevalence and high uninsured rates is particularly adverse, because uninsured individuals with chronic conditions delay seeking care until crises require emergency department visits. This pattern — documented in [KFF research](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/) on the health consequences of uninsurance — is most visible in border Texas and non-expansion South counties where both components are simultaneously elevated.

**Chronic disease + behavioral health.** Mental health conditions co-occur with chronic disease at high rates. Depression is approximately twice as common among people with diabetes as among the general population, according to [CDC research](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/managing/mental-health.html). Counties with both high chronic disease burden and limited behavioral health resources — including [Natrona County, WY](/county/56025) and [Franklin County, WA](/county/53021) — face a compounding challenge where treatment of each condition is complicated by the other.

**Chronic disease + perinatal outcomes.** Pre-gestational diabetes and hypertension are significant risk factors for adverse perinatal outcomes including preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal morbidity. Counties with elevated chronic disease prevalence among reproductive-age women — which includes many of the hotspot counties described above — show worse perinatal outcomes even controlling for provider access. This is the mechanism through which the chronic disease and perinatal outcomes components are correlated but not interchangeable within the composite.

## County-Level Strategy Implications

For health systems managing population health programs — particularly those with ACO contracts, value-based insurance arrangements, or CMMI participation — the county-level chronic disease data has direct operational relevance.

Attributing the full chronic disease burden to the service area requires combining [CDC PLACES prevalence estimates](https://www.cdc.gov/places/index.html) with population denominators from [ACS data](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs), then filtering by the specific conditions driving the health system's utilization and cost. Diabetes and heart failure are typically the largest drivers of preventable readmission and chronic care management program enrollment; hypertension management is the most cost-effective primary prevention target.

The Pulse Atlas chronic disease component score, at the county level, provides a normalized benchmark for comparing a health system's service area counties against national and state peers. Component-level detail is available through the individual county profiles referenced throughout this analysis, and the methodology page at [Pulse Atlas Methods](/methods) documents the exact CDC PLACES data vintage and normalization procedure.
