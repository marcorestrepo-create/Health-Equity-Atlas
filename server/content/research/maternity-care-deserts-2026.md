# Maternity Care Deserts: A County-Level Analysis of Access and Outcomes

More than one-third of all U.S. counties lack a single birthing facility or obstetric clinician — a condition that the [March of Dimes](https://www.marchofdimes.org/maternity-care-deserts-report) has defined as a "maternity care desert" and that the [University of Minnesota Rural Health Research Center](https://rhrc.umn.edu/publication/availability-of-hospital-based-obstetric-services-in-the-united-states-by-county-2010-2023-a-state-by-state-report/) has tracked through a separate lens of hospital-based obstetric services. The numbers represent a sustained structural failure in the U.S. maternal health infrastructure, and they are getting modestly worse over time on some measures even as others show marginal improvement.

## Definition and Measurement

A maternity care desert, as [March of Dimes defines it](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?top=23), is any county with no hospital or birth center offering obstetric care and no obstetric clinicians — including OB-GYNs, certified nurse midwives, and certified midwives. This is a threshold definition: the county either has zero of each, or it does not qualify as a desert.

The [March of Dimes classification system](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/reports/united-states/maternity-care-deserts) uses four tiers:

- **Maternity care desert:** No hospitals or birth centers offering obstetric care; no obstetric clinicians
- **Low access:** Fewer than two hospitals or birth centers, and fewer than 60 obstetric providers per 10,000 births, or uninsured rate above 10%
- **Moderate access:** Fewer than two hospitals or birth centers, or fewer than 60 obstetric providers per 10,000 births
- **Full access:** Two or more hospitals or birth centers, and at least 60 obstetric providers per 10,000 births, and uninsured rate below 10%

The [HRSA Maternity Care Target Area index](https://data.hrsa.gov/) provides a complementary continuous measure, incorporating chronic conditions, travel time to care, fertility rates, and insurance coverage in addition to provider and facility availability.

The maternal access component of the Pulse Atlas composite score (weighted at 11%) draws from both frameworks, reflecting both the binary desert designation and the continuous HRSA index. Full methodology is available at [Pulse Atlas Methods](/methods).

## Scale: How Many Counties, How Many Women

According to [April 2025 March of Dimes PeriStats data](https://www.marchofdimes.org/peristats/data?top=23):

- **1,104 counties** — more than 35% of all U.S. counties — are classified as maternity care deserts
- **More than 2.3 million women of reproductive age** live in maternity care deserts
- **More than 150,000 babies** were born to women living in those counties
- An additional **3.3 million women** live in counties with limited access

Between 2020 and 2022, the number of counties classified as deserts increased by roughly 2%, representing approximately 15,933 additional women with no maternity care, according to [March of Dimes reporting](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12096371/). The 2024 report showed a slight decrease in the percentage of counties classified as deserts (from 36% to 35%) while the absolute number of women living in deserts continued to rise, reflecting population distribution shifts.

The [University of Minnesota Rural Health Research Center](https://rhrc.umn.edu/publication/availability-of-hospital-based-obstetric-services-in-the-united-states-by-county-2010-2023-a-state-by-state-report/) tracks hospital-based obstetric services specifically, and their January 2026 report found that between 2010 and 2023, 269 of the 3,144 counties (8.6%) lost all hospital-based obstetric services. By 2023, 60% of rural counties and 38% of urban counties lacked any hospital-based obstetric services.

## Geographic Distribution

Maternity care deserts are not evenly distributed. The states with the highest percentage of counties classified as deserts include [North Dakota (73.6%), South Dakota (57.6%), Oklahoma (51.9%), Missouri (51.6%), Nebraska (51.3%), and Arkansas (50.7%)](https://www.risehealth.org/insights-articles/report-35-of-us-counties-are-maternity-care-deserts/), according to the 2024 March of Dimes report.

Nearly two-thirds of maternity care deserts are rural, according to [March of Dimes](https://www.marchofdimes.org/maternity-care-deserts-report). But the urban subset is not negligible — women living in urban maternity care deserts also experience significant travel burdens. [BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11080172/) found that the mean travel distance to the nearest obstetric facility was 28.1 miles for maternity care desert residents overall, compared to 8.3 miles nationally. Women in rural maternity care deserts averaged 33.4 miles; those in urban deserts averaged 25.0 miles.

American Indian/Alaska Native women face the most severe access deficits. Approximately 1 in 5 births among AI/AN women occur in counties without full maternity care access, according to [March of Dimes 2024 data](https://www.marchofdimes.org/maternity-care-deserts-report). [Research published in JAMA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7361647/) documents steady erosion of rural obstetric services over the prior decade.

## Outcomes: What Desert Status Predicts

Access deficits translate into measurable outcome differences. A 2022 March of Dimes finding, cited in [JAMA Family Medicine research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12096371/), noted that counties designated as maternity care deserts had a higher percentage of preterm births. Between 2020 and 2022, over 10,000 babies were born prematurely to women living in maternity care deserts, according to a [summary published by the BUILD Initiative](https://www.facebook.com/TheBuildInitiative/posts/1340060041487077/).

Maternal mortality, while measured at the state and national level rather than reliably at the county level, follows the same geographic gradient. [CDC maternal mortality data](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/maternal-mortality/index.htm) documents that the U.S. maternal mortality rate — approximately 22–25 deaths per 100,000 live births in recent years — masks enormous state and county variation, with the highest rates concentrated in the South and the rural Plains.

Counties with low telehealth access are 30% more likely to be maternity care deserts, according to [March of Dimes 2022 analysis](https://www.marchofdimes.org/sites/default/files/2022-10/2022_Maternity_Care_Report.pdf). This creates a compounding effect: geographic absence of in-person care is accompanied by infrastructural barriers to telehealth as a substitute.

## Five Case-Study Counties

**Comanche County, OK** ([Pulse Atlas profile](/county/40031)) sits in a state where Oklahoma has more than half its counties classified as maternity care deserts. While Comanche County, home to Lawton, has some provider infrastructure, it sits in a region surrounded by lower-access counties where women must travel significant distances for obstetric care. [Census QuickFacts](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/comanchecountyoklahoma/INC110223) show a poverty rate of 19.5% and an uninsured rate of 12.7% under age 65 — both above national averages — creating cost barriers that compound access barriers.

**Idaho County, ID** ([Pulse Atlas profile](/county/16049)) is among the counties in Idaho that have seen obstetric service losses. Idaho was among the states that lost obstetric services in 2 counties between 2022 and 2023, according to the [University of Minnesota Rural Health Research Center](https://rhrc.umn.edu/publication/availability-of-hospital-based-obstetric-services-in-the-united-states-by-county-2010-2023-a-state-by-state-report/). With a population that is largely rural and a geographic area that ranks among the largest counties in the contiguous United States, access to obstetric care requires substantial travel.

**Valley County, ID** ([Pulse Atlas profile](/county/16085)) presents the interaction of low population density, tourism-driven economics, and limited health infrastructure. The uninsured rate for Valley County has been estimated at approximately 10% in [ACS 5-year data](https://censusdepth.com/uninsured/counties/idaho), and its provider-to-population ratio for obstetric care reflects the challenges of sustaining specialized services in a small mountain county.

**Natrona County, WY** ([Pulse Atlas profile](/county/56025)) represents Wyoming's central corridor, where Casper anchors regional health services for surrounding counties that have no obstetric care. Wyoming's Medicaid non-expansion status has historically contributed to insurance gaps that complicate access for low-income pregnant women, though Wyoming has taken some steps toward expanding coverage.

**Franklin County, WA** ([Pulse Atlas profile](/county/53021)) illustrates a different pattern: a predominantly agricultural county with a substantial Hispanic farmworker population, where uninsured rates and language access barriers compound geographic distance from obstetric services.

## What Health Systems Can Do

The policy literature identifies several evidence-based approaches to addressing maternity care deserts. The [Commonwealth Fund](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/2021/sep/restoring-access-maternity-care-rural-america) synthesizes the evidence base:

**Medicaid expansion** remains the single largest lever for improving insurance coverage among reproductive-age women in non-expansion states. [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/status-of-state-medicaid-expansion-decisions/) documents the ongoing status of expansion decisions. Uninsured rates among women of childbearing age are substantially higher in non-expansion states, and the link between coverage and care-seeking behavior is well documented.

**Postpartum Medicaid extension to 12 months** — now available to states under the American Rescue Plan — addresses the cliff in coverage that has historically left postpartum women uninsured 60 days after delivery. Several states have adopted this option.

**Certified nurse-midwife scope of practice expansion** is a supply-side intervention that can extend obstetric care capacity in settings where physician availability is limited. States vary substantially in the scope of practice authority they grant CNMs.

**Telehealth prenatal protocols** can supplement in-person care, particularly for low-risk pregnancies where travel barriers are the primary constraint. The finding that telehealth-limited counties are 30% more likely to be maternity care deserts underscores the complementary nature of broadband infrastructure investment and maternal care access.

For hospital systems considering service line strategy in high-desert states, the county-level Pulse Atlas maternal access scores provide a fine-grained view of where the gap between need and supply is widest.
